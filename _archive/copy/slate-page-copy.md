## PAGE BODY

### Context

Random Labs is a Y Combinator-backed startup building Slate, a developer-native orchestration system for multi-agent coding. I came in during 2025, ahead of the product's public launch, to define what the brand should be and design a logo.

### Understanding the thing before drawing it

The first half of this work wasn't visual. I talked with one of the founders a lot, and from those conversations I wrote the brand foundation: what Slate is, why it exists, its origin story, mission, vision, and who it's for.

Mostly I was trying to understand them and their users well enough to know what their audience would be drawn to and what would put them off. Down to small things: aesthetics, tone, how the interface should feel.

I also wrote a tone and voice guide with a do-say and don't-say list, so the team could write consistently without me. The short version: **confident, not arrogant. Minimal, not dry. Tactile, not flashy. Built like a README, not a billboard.**

And I looked at how other agent and developer tools were positioning themselves, plus sites from brands I admired, to find where Slate could sit apart from them.

### Logomark

I explored ten directions, each built from a different reading of the product: composability as modular tiles, orchestration as conducting, the terminal as a surface, blank space as the thing you fill. Some were deliberately unconventional, included to test the edge of the brand's taste rather than to be chosen.

### Logotype and type system

Eight wordmark directions, from stencil and geometric constructions through to clean grotesques, tested at the sizes and contexts the mark would actually live in. Then a type system pairing heading and body faces with fallbacks, so it could be applied without me.

### Site design

A wireframe set for the marketing site: home in desktop and mobile, a getting-started flow in both, and a blog template. Plus a visual draft of the site itself.

### What I took from it

Two things.

Brand work depends on a team being ready to commit to a direction. I did align on positioning, but mostly with one founder. I took that to mean everyone was on board, and they weren't. Now I'd get every decision maker in the room early, not just my main point of contact.

And I'd cut the deck in half. Fifty-one pages is too many. I was excited and I had ideas, but volume isn't generosity. It puts the work of choosing onto the client. The strongest three directions, presented well, would have done more than ten.
